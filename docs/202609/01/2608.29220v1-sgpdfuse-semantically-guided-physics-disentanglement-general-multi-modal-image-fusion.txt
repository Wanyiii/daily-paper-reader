1 Introduction 

Multimodal Image Fusion (MMIF) aims to integrate complemen-tary information from different sensors into a single output that preserves essential scene content while suppressing sensor-specific degradations [11–15]. Taking infrared-visible fusion as a represen-tative example, the fused result is expected to highlight thermally salient targets while retaining the rich textures and semantic read-ability of visible images, thereby alleviating challenges such as low illumination, noise contamination, and sensor-specific artifacts. Meanwhile, fused images often serve as intermediate representa-tions for downstream perception tasks, and their quality directly affects the robustness of subsequent systems such as object detec-tion and segmentation. Most existing deep fusion methods treat fusion as a signal ag-gregation problem: given source images {𝐼 𝐴 , 𝐼 𝐵 }, the objective is to learn a mapping that maximizes information preservation through weighted summation or feature concatenation. We argue that this formulation suffers from an inherent limitation: it fundamentally conflates two distinct signal components—the intrinsic physical properties of the scene (true temperature, surface reflectance, sharp texture) and the variation factors introduced by environmental conditions (thermal reflections, illumination changes, optical blur). This conflation manifests as persistent artifacts: ghost thermal tar-gets on reflective surfaces, color distortions from exposure imbal-ance, or texture loss in defocused regions. Consequently, exist-ing methods struggle to simultaneously preserve essential content while suppressing environmental interference. To address this, we propose a physics-disentanglement perspec-tive by revisiting the fusion objective itself. We draw inspiration from established physical imaging models: in infrared imaging, the observed radiance 𝐿 𝑜𝑏𝑠 = 𝜖𝐵 (𝑇 ) + ( 1 − 𝜖 )𝐿 𝑒𝑛𝑣 comprises thermal emission and environmental reflection [1]; in photography, the Retinex model 𝐼 = 𝑅 · 𝐿 separates reflectance from illumination [8]; in optics, the defocus model 𝐼 = 𝑆 ⊗ 𝐾 distinguishes sharp con-tent from blur [24, 26]. These diverse physical processes share a unified structure: 𝐼 = G( Intrinsic , Variation ). This suggests that fusion should not blindly aggregate signals, but rather disentangle intrinsic content from variation factors before merging. Motivated by this insight, we propose SGPDFuse (Semantically-Guided Physics-Disentanglement Multi-Modal Image Fusion), a unified framework that bridges high-level semantics with low-level physics. Our key observation is that semantic features from vision foundation models—particularly DINOv3 [25]—encode material properties and object identities that are invariant to physical varia-tions, making them ideal for predicting the variation parameters ( 𝜖 ,illumination quality, focus measure) needed for disentanglement. We instantiate this via a Semantic-Physical Parametric Bridge (SPPB) that uses LoRA-adapted [7] DINOv3 features to predict the variation confidence map W, which drives Intrinsic-Variation disentangle-ment of both base (low-frequency) and detail (high-frequency) fea-tures. To guide the decomposition, we further introduce a Semantic Alignment mechanism that explicitly anchors the fused representa-tion to salient semantic features via cosine similarity, while enforc-ing physical texture fidelity through Gram-matrix regularization to eliminate unnatural artifacts. 2 Related Work 2.1 Task-Specific Multi-Modal Image Fusion 

Deep learning has driven remarkable progress in task-specific im-age fusion. Early CNN-based methods such as DenseFuse [9] and U2Fusion [34] established encoder-decoder paradigms for feature aggregation, while RFN-Nest [10] introduced residual feedback mechanisms. Subsequent methods further specialized their designs for distinct fusion scenarios: IVIF approaches [16, 22, 41] incorpo-rate detection networks and adversarial training to preserve ther-mal saliency, MEF and MFF techniques [38, 39] leverage exposure-specific brightness decomposition and focus-map guidance, and PIAFusion [27] exploits illumination-aware progressive training. Transformer architectures have further extended modeling capacity, with SwinFusion [21] capturing cross-modal long-range dependen-cies and CDDFuse [42] decomposing features into base and detail branches via transformer blocks. More recently, Text-IF [36], TC-MoA [47], RFfusion [31], diffusion-based [44], and task-driven [20] approaches further push the frontier via text conditioning, adapter mixtures, iterative denoising, and detection-aware strategies. Despite their diversity, all these methods aggregate features from raw source signals, treating intrinsic scene content and extrinsic environmental factors as equally valid fusion targets. This leads to artifacts when environmental interference is present, such as ghost thermal targets on reflective surfaces, texture loss in defocused regions, or unnatural halos under extreme exposure conditions. 

2.2 Physics-Informed and Disentanglement-Based Vision 

Explicit physical modeling has proven effective within individual imaging domains. HADAR [1] leverages the Stefan–Boltzmann radiation law 𝐿 = 𝜖𝐵 (𝑇 ) + ( 1 − 𝜖 )𝐿 env to disentangle thermal emis-sion from environmental reflection in longwave infrared imaging. Algorithm-unrolling methods [45] also exploit physical models to drive network architecture design. Retinex theory [8] decomposes images as 𝐼 = 𝑅 · 𝐿 (reflectance times illumination), spawning a family of deep enhancement methods [32] for low-light and ex-posure correction. Depth-from-Defocus [26] and learning-based defocus deblurring [24] model the blurred image as 𝐼 = 𝑆 ⊗ 𝐾 ,recovering the all-in-focus image 𝑆 by estimating the blur kernel 

𝐾 . In representation learning, disentangled generative models [2] aim to separate content from style, but these are trained for single-modality synthesis rather than cross-modal fusion. These approaches share a common mathematical structure: the observed signal is a composite of intrinsic scene properties and extrinsic variation factors. While each of these approaches demon-strates the power of physical priors, they remain confined to single tasks, require domain-specific supervision (hyperspectral emissiv-ity measurements, paired exposure data, depth ground truth). Crit-ically, the challenge of estimating physical variation parameters from entangled multi-modal observations—without any explicit physical measurements—remains entirely unaddressed.. 

Our Perspective. We observe that vision foundation models bridge this gap : DINO [4, 23, 25] semantic features encode material properties and structural priors invariant to the physical varia-tions governing each imaging modality, enabling unified Intrinsic-Variation decomposition across diverse fusion tasks without explicit physical supervision. 

3 Intrinsic-Variation Structure in Multi-Modal Imaging 

A fundamental but underappreciated property of multi-modal imag-ing is that every observed modality is a composite signal—it mixes the stable physical properties of the scene with transient, sensor-specific interference. Existing fusion methods operate directly on these composite observations, implicitly assuming that sophisti-cated feature aggregation can filter out the undesired components. We establish the theoretical foundation for SGPDFuse by formaliz-ing a structural property that is universally present in multi-modal imaging yet has been overlooked in existing fusion methods.

4 Methodology 

We present SGPDFuse, illustrated in Fig. 3. Grounded in the two-axis factorization of Eq. (3), the framework processes each source pair through a shared dual-branch encoder that extracts base and detail features, predicts the physical variation confidence map W

via the Semantic-Physical Parametric Bridge (SPPB), disentangles intrinsic components Φ from variation components Ψ at each fre-quency scale, and fuses only Φ through physics-guided aggregation. Training follows a two-stage curriculum: encoder–decoder recon-struction pre-training to establish a stable feature space, followed by end-to-end fusion fine-tuning with the full loss.

These quantities are notoriously difficult to measure directly, yet DINOv3 patch tokens encode them implicitly: activations for metallic sur-faces differ systematically from those for vegetation, reflecting distinct physical material properties. The SPPB makes this corre-spondence explicit through a physics-grounded projection.

Complementary Source Assignment. We assign W𝐴 = W and 

W𝐵 = 1−W , encoding a physically motivated complementarity derived from the image formation models. In IVIF, the radiation law 𝐿 = 𝜖𝐵 (𝑇 ) + ( 1−𝜖 )𝐿 env directly induces this duality: at a pixel where emissivity 𝜖 is high, the IR channel is dominated by genuine emission ( Φ for source 𝐴 ), while the visible channel captures mostly reflected illumination ( Ψ for source 𝐵 ); at low-𝜖 surfaces, the roles reverse. In MEF and MFF, the complementarity follows from en-ergy conservation across exposure pairs and from focus-defocus duality, respectively. We acknowledge this is an approximation; at pixels where both modalities carry distinct intrinsic information, the learnable residual correctors RΦ, RΨ adaptively refine the de-composition where the hard assignment is insufficient. At both base and detail scales, Ψ components are discarded before fusion, eliminating variation by construction.

5 Experiments 5.1 Experimental Setup 

Experiment datasets. To evaluate the performance of our method, we conduct experiments on three representative image fusion tasks: infrared and visible image fusion (IVIF), multi-exposure image fu-sion (MEF) and multi-focus image fusion (MFF). For IVIF, we use three widely adopted benchmarks: M 3FD [16], RoadScene [35], and TNO [28]. For MEF and MFF, we utilize the MEFB [39] and MFIF [40] datasets. For downstream evaluation on IVIF, we use M 3FD [16] for object detection and FMB [17] for semantic segmentation. 

Implementation Details. Input images are resized to the nearest 16 × multiple of their original resolution; test images are evaluated at full resolution without any post-processing. The shared encoder uses 𝑑 =64 feature channels with 𝑁 =4 NAFNet blocks; the SPPB uses 32 hidden channels per branch. We adopt DINOv3 ViT-S/16 (𝑑 dino =384) fine-tuned via LoRA with rank 𝑟 =32 and 𝛼 =64. Both stages use AdamW (weight decay 1 ×10 −4) with cosine annealing (𝜂 min =10 −6) and batch size 2. Stage 1 (reconstruction pre-training): 50 epochs, lr =1×10 −4, ℓ1+SSIM loss. Stage 2 (end-to-end fusion): 100 epochs, lr =5×10 −5; loss coefficients 𝜆 decomp =2.0, 𝜆 tv =5.0, 𝜆 𝑔 =10 .0, 

𝜆 RecA =0.1. For IVIF, the SPPB scale and bias are initialized from the emissivity database derived from HADAR [1]. All experiments are conducted on 8 NVIDIA RTX3090 (24GB) GPUs using PyTorch. 

Evaluation Metrics. We adopt four complementary metrics from IVIF-ZOO [18]: Correlation Coefficient (CC), Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Transfer Entropy (TE), and Multi-Scale Structural Similarity (MS-SSIM, reported as SSIM in Table 1). For downstream tasks, we use YOLOv8s [29] with mAP 50:95 for object detection and SegFormer-B1 [33] with mIoU for semantic segmentation. For downstream IVIF application, input images are resized to 256 × 256.

5.4 Ablation Studies 

5.4.1 Study on SPPB. As shown in Table 3, we ablate three aspects of the SPPB design. Replacing the physics-grounded confidence map with a uniform weight ( W ≡ 0.5, variant B) leads to consis-tent degradation across all metrics, confirming that fixed isotropic weighting fails to distinguish intrinsic from variation content. Re-moving the Intrinsic-Variation disentanglement entirely (variant C) causes further drops, demonstrating that the W-weighted projec-tion is essential for suppressing modality-specific artifacts before fusion. Freezing DINOv3 without LoRA adaptation (variant D) also hurts performance, indicating that task-aware fine-tuning is neces-sary for material-sensitive emissivity estimation. 

5.4.2 Study on RecA Loss. We further ablate the components of 

LRecA in Table 3. Removing LRecA entirely (variant E) degrades structural fidelity, confirming that DINOv3 feature-space align-ment provides an essential semantic anchor during fusion training. Ablating only Lcontent (variant F) causes semantic drift toward a single modality, while removing only Lstyle (variant G) introduces texture incoherence in the fused output. These results validate that content and style alignment are complementary and jointly necessary for producing perceptually consistent fusion results. 

6 Conclusion 

We propose SGPDFuse, which replaces blind feature aggregation 

with physics-disentangled representations via a Semantic-Physical Parametric Bridge, separating invariant scene attributes from tran-sient environmental factors under the Intrinsic-Variation principle. Building on this decomposition, the physics-guided fusion oper-ates exclusively on intrinsic components, with Semantic Alignment via cosine similarity and Gram-matrix regularization providing self-consistent supervision in the DINOv3 feature space. Extensive experiments demonstrate consistent state-of-the-art performance within a single unified architecture.
