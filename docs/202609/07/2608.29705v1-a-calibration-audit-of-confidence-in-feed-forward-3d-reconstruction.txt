## Abstract 

Feed-forward 3D reconstruction models emit a per-pixel confidence that downstream systems read as a reliability signal. It is trained as a loss weight, not as an uncertainty magnitude, and whether it can be used as an error predic-tion has not been measured. We audit seven released back-bones on thirteen datasets and score the confidence on four arXiv:2608.29705v1 [cs.CV] 30 Aug 2026 properties, how well it ranks error, whether its level is right on average, whether it holds across the confidence range, and whether its intervals cover the truth. The confidence ranks error well, but the predicted uncertainty is too low when it is read under conditions that are not exactly those of training. The median case is off by 2.4× across all seven models, and the error prediction is further off the more con-fident the model is. We show that this phenomenon can ap-pear even though the loss’s optimum is reached. A released model resumed under its own loss reaches that optimum on its training data within a few hundred updates and stays overconfident on unseen frames. A power law with two con-stants per backbone and dataset corrects the overall magni-tude of the predicted uncertainty and leaves the ranking un-touched. What no rescaling reaches is the scene, which we attribute to the model’s missing knowledge of scale across predictions. Every correction we tried is close to right on average and still leaves two thirds of held-out scenes out-side a five-point band, because what a scene is missing is a shape rather than a shift. We release the audit protocol, its results, and the fitted constants per model and dataset. Fitted with the target dataset held out, the constants bring the median case from 2.4× off to 1.35 ×, and a refit on a few labelled scenes of that dataset reaches 1.12 ×.

## 1. Introduction 

Feed-forward models reconstruct 3D geometry from uncal-ibrated images in a single pass and emit a per-pixel confi-dence alongside it [36, 55–57], a lineage surveyed in [61]. That confidence is the only reliability signal these models ship, and downstream systems use it. They threshold points before fusion, weight residuals in bundle adjustment, or set the measurement noise of a filter. It is trained as a weight in a confidence-weighted regres-sion loss, not as an error bar, and practice already reflects the difference. SLAM3R thresholds two DUSt3R-style heads at 1.5 and at 12 [39]. FILT3R runs a Kalman filter on a frozen CUT3R, adapts the process noise, and leaves the measurement noise a fixed scalar [23]. DeepFactors com-putes a weight from a predicted standard deviation and then returns one instead [10]. The one visual-inertial system we found that does use a learned uncertainty as a covariance first fits a constant gain on a validation set, so that the mean Mahalanobis distance comes out at one [25]. Every one of these choices avoids relying on the confidence’s level. It is consumed as a ranking, and where a level is needed it comes from elsewhere. Whether that caution is justified, and by how much, has not been measured. The field’s answer has been to replace the head. Eviden-tial regression [1, 62], heteroscedastic regression [29, 41], MC dropout [13] and deep ensembles [32] all produce a predictive distribution, and recent work reports large gains in confidence ranking on these backbones [62]. That is the right answer when retraining is possible. Two things remain. Released checkpoints still ship the original head, and those checkpoints are what systems are built on. And the problem is not an undertrained head. We resume a released model under its own loss, and it reaches that loss’s optimum on the data it is training on within a few hundred updates. A head retrained the same way is overconfident on frames it has not seen. We therefore ask what the native confidence is worth as a level, and how much of the gap a user can close without touching the weights. The loss is minimised where the confidence equals a constant over the expected residual (Sec. 3), so inverting the trained relation returns the mean residual of training , measured against the target the loss used, over the data it was trained on, in the units it was written in. A deployed model is read against ground truth, on new data, in the scene’s own units. We measure the resulting error on 168 settings (Fig. 1) over seven released backbones and thirteen datasets at full pixel population (2.786 × 10 10 scored pixels). We then measure how much of it post-hoc recalibration recovers.

## 2. Related Work 

Dense uncertainty and how it is scored. Per-pixel un-certainty in dense prediction is conventionally scored by how well it ranks error. Sparsification error and its sum-mary AUSE come from optical flow [22] and are standard in monocular depth [46]. Stereo confidence is scored the same way, through ROC curves [47]. Whether the number is the right level is rarely asked. The exceptions change the training objective to make it so [8, 40], while label-free post-hoc methods improve ranking instead of correct-ing a scale [16, 21]. One independent study reaches our direction from outside, reporting that VGGT’s depth confi-dence “exhibits the typical phenomenon of overconfidence” on DTU [20], though on a scale-free proxy, so it corrobo-rates the sign and not the size. 

Replacing the head. Recent work replaces the loss weight with a head that is a predictive distribution by construction. The options are evidential regression with a closed-form Student-t posterior [1, 62], heteroscedastic regression [29, 41], MC dropout [13] and deep ensem-bles [32]. Trust3R reports 25 % lower AURC and 41 % lower AUSE on ScanNet++ [62]. We take that as settled and disjoint from what we measure, since those are ranking metrics on a retrained head and ours are level metrics on the native one. Whether a differently trained head has the right level is an open question we can only bound from outside the family. Sec. 5 scores an uncertainty read off sample dis-agreement and a direct error regressor on the same grid, and both miss, in opposite directions. 

Post-hoc recalibration. Rescaling a frozen model’s un-certainty is standard [18, 31, 37, 45]. The published form for regression is a single global scalar fitted by likelihood on a labelled validation set [33]. The same two correc-tions appear in the conformal literature as split conformal prediction [35, 43] and its per-group refinement [5, 54]. What such a guarantee can be conditioned on is settled the-ory [4, 34, 54], and the methods that pursue conditional cov-erage where it is attainable act on pre-specified groups [24], kernel neighbourhoods [17], a covariate shift between cali-bration and test [51], or a target stated as a risk [3]. Sec. 6 runs the first four, and the risk-control target is in the supplement. Conformal constructions have reached dense per-pixel regression [2] and geometric estimation [58], but not, as far as we know, monocular depth or feed-forward pointmap reconstruction. 

Uncertainty in geometric systems. The loss we analyse descends from learned loss attenuation, where a predicted variance down-weights hard pixels during training [29]. That a frozen geometric system’s uncertainty is systemati-cally wrong and correctable offline has precedent in visual-inertial odometry [52], and overconfidence has been diag-nosed in semantic 3D mapping and traced to the fusion rule [9].

## 4. Audit Protocol 

Setup. We decode the uncertainty as ˆσ = α/C and match the target and the normalization to the ones each model trained with, wherever the release permits (supplement, Tab. S1). A setting is one backbone’s output head on one dataset, and each is scored on the four properties of Tab. 1. They answer different questions, so we never summarize them into one number. 

Grid. Six of the seven backbones expose two heads and one a single head, giving 26 × 6 + 12 = 168 settings over 13 datasets. These are ScanNet (val, train) [11], TUM (static, dynamic) [50], 7-Scenes [49], BlendedMVS [59], Bonn [42], KITTI [14, 53], CO3Dv2 [48], DL3DV [38], Sintel [6], Virtual KITTI 2 [7] and Dynamic Replica [26], the same for every backbone. We sample eight-frame clips, so scene, clip and frame are three nested units we can read the same statistic on. Some datasets are in a model’s training diet, but the pixels scored are never those training saw. Three of the 13 are close relatives of others, so they amount to ten independent sources, and one backbone cannot run CO3Dv2. Every setting is scored at full pixel population, 2.786 × 10 10 pixels, a pixel counting when its ground truth is valid, its depth is in range, and it falls inside the model’s field of view. 

Metrics. Ranking is scored by the Spearman correlation ρ

between ˆσ and e, with a setting passing at ρ ≥ 0.10 . We re-port two, on different populations. One pools every scored pixel. The other correlates inside each image and takes the median over the setting’s images, which is what rules out a confidence tracking only the per-image error scale. The second runs on fewer pixels, dropping the confidence floor and the out-of-range bins, a median of 7 % of a setting and 81 % on the worst, so the two are not one statistic at two granularities.

Level. The level is another matter. On the five backbones trained against ground truth, every confidence bin that fails does so by claiming too little error, 1,224 of them, and not one the other way. Across the grid the median setting is off by 2.4×, across these five by 2.8×. StreamVGGT, trained against a teacher and read against that teacher, is the one backbone close to calibrated, though on that target it still fails high on 40 bins and low on 10 (Tab. 2). Read against ground truth, it joins the others. VGGT’s α is a function default, so its bins are reported and not counted. The five that remain descend from one loss, so the count is a census over a single lineage and not five independent tests. Fig. 2 shows one held-out frame of two settings, where the uncertainty tracks the error’s structure and sits below it almost everywhere. 

Slope. The predictions are also spread wider than the errors (Fig. 1c). The calibration curve’s slope b lies entirely below one on 123 of 168 settings. The predicted error at a setting’s 90th percentile of confidence is about a sixth of that at its 10th percentile. A slope below one means the model grows more overconfident as its confidence rises, so the predictions a reader would weight most heavily are the ones furthest off. Part of the flatness is aggregation rather than a flat head, since a setting whose scenes are each cali-brated at their own level still fits at 0.93 . Two checks bound that. Restricting to the ground-truth settings leaves 114 of 142 below one, and fitting inside single frames, where no aggregation is possible, puts the slope below one on all 54 settings of the four backbones where the fit is available. The headline count is the grid one, 123 of 168, and the other two say the effect survives its most likely artifact. 

Coverage. On the five backbones trained against ground truth the same overconfidence appears in the intervals, which are too narrow at all eleven nominal levels and pro-portionally worst at the tight end, where MASt3R’s nominal 

10 % interval holds 0.6 % of the truth (Fig. 1d). 

Domains and units. Sorting datasets by indoor or outdoor, static or dynamic, real or synthetic ground truth moves the level by at most 1.6×, and datasets outside every training diet read no worse than those inside. What moves it is the normalization a dataset was trained under. Read in metres, CUT3R is 3.0× off on the datasets it trained on with metric residuals and 15 .6× on those with scene-normalized ones. The spread across a backbone’s datasets is that, not scene content, and the supplement gives the level of every setting and marks its branch. 

Convergence. A head at the optimum of Sec. 3 would sat-isfy Eq. 3, so every confidence bin would deliver the error it predicts. No backbone does. Either training never reached that optimum or one of its three conditions moved, and the first is directly testable (supplement). A fresh confidence head on DUSt3R’s trunk, retrained on two of DUSt3R’s own datasets, is calibrated on the frames it trains on and overconfident on frames it does not see. The released DUSt3R put back into training under the same loss behaves the same way. Read on CO3Dv2, a dataset in its diet, it starts 2.79 × off. That reading is already under changed conditions, since the clips, crops and sampler are ours and not the release’s, and the pixels are ones training never saw (Sec. 4). It reaches 1.10 × after roughly 300 updates on that draw, and stays within 0.91 to 1.12 × over ten further epochs under both learning-rate schedules. So the loss reaches its optimum on whatever draw it is currently training on, and the level a reader sees is set by which draw that is rather than by how long the model trained. A backbone trained further under the same loss would, on this evidence, stop at the same place. The miss is in the loss’s target, normaliza-tion and training population, not in how far the checkpoint sits from its own optimum. On this evidence a few hundred updates under the same loss on labelled scenes of the target domain would repair the level there, though we ran it only on training data. ## 6. Post-hoc Recalibration 

The correction class. The weights are frozen, so the only thing left to adjust is the reported uncertainty. A correction rescales it as a function of the confidence value and the dataset. Every published rescaling of a frozen model’s uncertainty is in that class. The conformal methods that condition on more re-choose the interval instead (Tab. 3, middle block). One scalar for the whole dataset is split conformal prediction [35, 43], and it is what temperature scaling [18] and σ-scaling [33] do here. Splitting the pixels by confidence bin and giving each bin its own scalar is the same correction at a finer grain [5, 54]. Nothing else about a pixel enters a rescaling. A correction that repairs one property often damages another, so Tab. 3 scores every one we tried on all four at once. 

The two constants. Two things are wrong, and they need different repairs. The level is off, so the average bin claims the wrong error by a factor, and the slope is below one, so the bins are wrong relative to each other. One number for the whole dataset fixes the level and nothing else, since it multiplies every bin alike and moves only the intercept of Eq. 5. A separate number per bin fixes both, but it damages the ranking. A power law fixes both and leaves the ranking alone. Eq. 5 fits ln ¯ e = c + b ln ˆ σ, so multiplying by 

g(ˆ σ) = ec ˆσ b−1 gives ˆσ′ = ec ˆσ b. (6) Writing s = b − 1 and a = c + s ln α puts this in the form we release, ˆσ′ = ˆ σ exp( a + s ln(ˆ σ/α )) , where a sets the level and 1 + s the slope. Over all 168 settings on held-out scenes it takes the median setting from 2.4× off to 1.11 ×,and the slope from 0.65 to 0.99 .

Ranking. A power law cannot change the ranking, since the map ˆσ 7 → ec ˆσ b is strictly increasing whenever the ex-ponent is positive. The fitted exponent is positive on 162 of 168 settings, and negative but within 0.07 of zero on the rest. We clamp any exponent below 0.05 , which affects ten settings. The within-image ρ is then identical to the native one, by measurement on the 158 settings we leave alone and by construction on the ten we clamp. The same repair applied bin by bin does cost ranking, because a step correction can reorder pixels at a bin edge even where the smooth one cannot. Estimating it without labels is the open question, and we could not answer it. Ground truth on an opening window of each scene, applied to the rest, never beats a correction that ignores the scene. Labels from inside the corrected window do help, but the gain survives shuffling the frames into a random order, so it is averaging noise rather than following anything. 

Conformal baselines. Four published methods condi-tion on more than the bin alone, by a richer partition of frames [54], by overlapping groups [24], by distance to the test frame [17], or by a density ratio between calibration and deployment [51]. Run against the same per-bin base-line on all 168 settings, none moves the median off zero (Tab. 3). Each improves 15 to 36 % of settings and damages a similar share, and adaptive conformal prediction [15] does no better when given ground truth on an opening window of up to half the frames. The last of the four is the informative one, because the shift it corrects is really there. A classifier separates cal-ibration frames from held-out frames at AUC 0.68 using the predictions alone, yet correcting for that shift leaves as many settings worse as better. What differs between cali-bration and deployment is not which frames are drawn but what the error does at a given confidence. None of this is ruled out in advance. Exact coverage can-not be promised at a single point in feature space [4, 34, 54], but a scene is a group of 10 5 to 10 6 pixels rather than a point. Whether what is missing varies smoothly with something observable about a scene we tested and could not settle, with too few scenes per setting to tell a real effect from none. 

Outside the class. Two repairs sit outside it. A linear probe on the model’s frozen features, rather than on its confidence value alone, closes a median 0.94 of the slope gap out of sample on the six of nine settings of three backbones that pass a validity screen. What transfers there is the level and the slope, not the ranking. Expressing the prediction in the frame’s own predicted scale needs no labels and recovers half of the remaining coverage gap on MASt3R’s metric branch ( n = 14 ), against a transductive ceiling and leaving the non-metric branch where it was. Improving the ranking needs a different head on the same features [62]. The protocol, the per-setting outputs and the fitted constants accompany this submission. 

## 7. Conclusion 

Native confidence is a usable ranking signal with an unre-liable level. It ranks error inside images almost everywhere and understates that error, the more so the more confident the model is. Post-hoc repair recovers the level at no cost to the ranking, once the correction is written as a power law rather than a step function, and not much further.
